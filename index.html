People's historySun 20 SepFirst walk

Yandai Xiejie 烟袋斜街

Hamama’s find, and he is right that it is next door. 232 metres that have changed class three times.

The practical bitThree minutes from the hotel. Walk it east to west and you come out at Yinding Bridge on Houhai, which is the evening handed to you.

Two hundred and thirty-two metres, running north-east to south-west off Di’anmen Outer Street, which is why it is called 斜 — slanting. It does not obey the imperial grid, and the name is a joke about its shape: the long street is the stem of a pipe, the east entrance the mouthpiece, and the west end, curving south towards Yinding Bridge, the bowl.

It is named for what it sold and for whom. The Manchu banner garrison was quartered in the north of the city, and banner households smoked — dry tobacco and water tobacco — so the shopkeepers here made and sold the pouches and long-stemmed pipes they needed. The Guangxu-era Shuntian Prefecture Gazetteer records the street under this name. Under the Qing it was a high-end strip for officials and nobility, with houses like Huixianlou and Qingyunlou on it, and ordinary Beijingers did not shop here.

Then the dynasty fell. After the 1912 abdication the aristocracy began selling its possessions, and the antique dealers moved in — Baowenzhai, Minwenzhai — turning a court street into a second-hand market where you could buy the empire by the piece. That is the second class regime on the same 232 metres.

The third arrived in November 2010, when Yandai Xiejie was named one of China’s Historical and Cultural Famous Streets and Shichahai was earmarked as a showcase for “Humanistic Beijing”. The shopfronts were restored to a Qing look and the trade became souvenirs, snacks and coffee for visitors. So you are walking a street built for a garrison caste, repurposed to liquidate that caste’s property, and finally restored as a picture of itself. Guangfu Temple, Ming, is still standing on it, protected, in among the fridge magnets.

The practical bitUniqlo's flagship is in Taikoo Li West. 25 min by taxi from the hotel — do it straight off the airport run if you land early enough, then go back to Houhai for the evening.

Sanlitun was the embassy quarter's bar street: through the 1980s and 90s a strip of dive bars serving diplomats, foreign students and anyone else who could get in, and by the 2000s the centre of Beijing nightlife. People's historyMon 21 SepMorning

Panjiayuan flea market 潘家园旧货市场

Where the Mao era is sold off by the kilo: badges, posters, and dead people's letters.

The practical bitWeekdays 08:30–18:00, permanent stalls only. The real market is the weekend ground sellers from 04:30, and your only weekend morning is arrival day.

The largest secondhand market in China, and the main place the material of the Cultural Revolution still changes hands: badges, Little Red Books, posters, porcelain Maos, and — the part worth your time — the paper. Family letters, diaries, work permits, self-criticisms and photograph albums, cleared out of flats when an old person dies and sold by weight. Fan Jianchuan built much of the Red Era collection you will see in Chengdu out of markets like this one. Most of the 'antiques' are reproductions and everyone knows it; the paper is usually real and costs a few yuan. What you are looking at is what happened to the physical record of a mass political movement once nobody official wanted to keep it.

Landmark, reframedMon 28 SepLate afternoon

Daci Temple and Fangsuo 大慈寺 · 方所

A Tang monastery with a luxury mall built round it, and a bookshop in the basement imitating the monastery's library.

The practical bitFangsuo is on B1 at Taikoo Li, units M68-70, open 10:00-22:00 and to 22:30 at weekends. The temple courtyard is free and closes earlier than the shops. Chunxi Road, for the clothes, is the next street north, and IFS is the tower with the panda hauling itself over the parapet.

The temple came first, and a Korean built it. Wuxiang was born in 684, the third son of King Seongdeok of Silla, and Chengdu knew him simply as the Kim monk. He reached Chang'an in 728, was received by the emperor, and spent the rest of his life in Sichuan. He planned and supervised Daci Temple, finished in 757: ninety-six courtyards, more than ten thousand Buddha images and over a thousand wall paintings, the largest monastery in the southwest. A Song-dynasty account of its paintings counts 8,524 bays. Local people called it the Prince's Temple after him.

You will be told that Xuanzang was ordained here before he set out for India. The dates do not allow it. He took full ordination in 622 and this temple was built 135 years later; he was ordained somewhere in Chengdu, most likely at Duobao Temple, and the story migrated to the more famous address, which is what stories do. Worth knowing, because it is the claim the site sells itself on.

In 2015 Swire Properties and Sino-Ocean finished 219,000 square metres of luxury mall around the surviving temple. Taikoo Li is genuinely well made — low grey lanes instead of a box, six restored heritage buildings, slow lanes for the teahouses and fast lanes for the flagship stores. The temple is now the development's heritage credential, the thing that lets a shopping centre be described as urban regeneration. You can walk from incense in the courtyard to a Gucci window in ninety seconds, which makes the argument without anyone having to state it.

Then go down to B1. Fangsuo opened on 29 January 2015: 5,500 square metres, nine-metre ceilings, raw concrete, one of the longest bookshelves in China. The Taiwanese designer Chu Chih-Kang built it as a copy of a 藏经阁, the scripture repository of a Buddhist monastery — a copy, in other words, of the real thing standing two minutes away at ground level. It was funded by Mao Jihong, who made his money from the fashion label Exception, and it gets named one of the most beautiful bookshops in the world about once a year. It is also the flagship of what Chinese critics call 网红书店, the influencer bookstore, where the product being sold is the photograph. In 2020 staff at the Xi'an branch turned away a man in camouflage trousers, having decided he was a migrant worker, and the row went national. A cultural commons with a dress code. Go and look at it anyway — it is the most beautiful room on this list, and it is worth knowing who it was made beautiful for.

Chunxi Road, the shopping street immediately north, was driven through the old city in 1924 on the orders of Yang Sen, the warlord then running Chengdu, who wanted a modern commercial avenue and demolished what stood in the way to get one. It has been the city's shopping street ever since, and it is where the clothes are. Give the whole block an hour, and keep the contrast in hand: you are eating in Yulin in three hours. Same city, entirely different economy.

The practical bitAcross the river from the hotel. Cross by the Dongshuimen bridge on foot, and walk back over it for the cave hotpot.

Britain forced Chongqing open as a treaty port in 1891, and the French, Americans and Japanese followed; the south bank filled with consulates, missions, and the Imperial Maritime Customs, run by foreigners collecting China's own tariffs. The buildings are preserved along Nanbin Road and in the Ciyun Temple area. A quiet walk with a river view before dinner, and the last stop before your late flight.

Cave hotpot 洞子火锅

Chongqing hotpot inside a wartime bomb shelter. Full red broth, tripe, duck intestine.

The practical bitDongting Hotpot 洞亭火锅 near the hotel is one; any 洞子 restaurant works. Order 特辣. Walk back over the Dongshuimen bridge from Nanbin Road; you need to be at the hotel by 22:30.

Chongqing hotpot began on the docks: porters and boatmen cooked cheap offal in a fiery broth of beef tallow, chilli and Sichuan pepper. After the war, dozens of the city's air-raid shelters became restaurants, and 'cave hotpot' is now the way locals eat it: a long stone tunnel, sweat, and the loudest room you'll be in all trip. The last dinner of the trip, eaten in a tunnel, which is the right place to end it.
